The Changing Face of Golf Travel — Trends Reshaping Who Travels and How

← All Articles

Golf travel in 2025 looks different than it did a decade ago. The demographics are shifting, the trip formats are diversifying, and the expectations around what constitutes a "golf trip" have expanded well beyond the traditional 16-guys-and-72-holes model. Understanding these trends helps travelers find experiences that actually fit — rather than defaulting to formats designed for someone else.

Women's Golf Travel Is Growing Fast

Women now represent roughly 28% of on-course golfers in the United States — approximately 8 million players — and that percentage has been rising steadily since 2020. The travel industry is responding: women-only golf retreats, mother-daughter trips, and couples' itineraries that balance golf with other activities are among the fastest-growing segments. Resorts that historically marketed to a male-dominated audience are recalibrating — offering more flexible tee time structures, expanding spa and wellness programming, and adjusting group packages to accommodate diverse group compositions. The practical takeaway: if a trip format doesn't fit, it's increasingly easy to find one that does.

Solo Golf Travel Is Having a Moment

The idea of traveling alone to play golf was once considered unusual. Today, it's one of the fastest-growing trip formats — driven partly by remote work flexibility, partly by the increasing difficulty of coordinating group schedules, and partly by a growing recognition that solo travel offers a different, often deeper kind of experience. Solo travelers report higher satisfaction with course selection (no compromises), pace of play (no waiting on group dynamics), and overall trip quality. Most courses now accommodate single bookings routinely, and many resorts offer solo-friendly packages that waive single-supplement fees during off-peak periods.

Golf + Wellness Is No Longer an Afterthought

The traditional golf trip structure — 36 holes, heavy meals, late nights — is being rebalanced. A growing number of travelers are structuring trips around morning golf, afternoon recovery (spa, yoga, hiking), and lighter evening routines. Resorts in desert destinations (Palm Springs, Scottsdale, Las Vegas) have been early adopters of this model, integrating spa programming directly into golf packages. The result is a format that's physically sustainable across multiple days and appealing to a broader range of travelers — especially couples and mixed-interest groups where not everyone plays 18 holes every day.

Multi-Generational Trips Are the New Family Reunion

Grandparents, parents, and adult children traveling together is one of the most significant shifts in the broader travel industry, and golf is positioned to benefit disproportionately. A well-structured multi-generational trip might include 18 holes for the serious golfers, a 9-hole or par-3 course for the casual players, and non-golf activities (spa, sightseeing, dining) for the non-golfers — all within the same resort or region. The key operational challenge is variety: the destination needs enough course diversity and enough non-golf programming to keep different generations engaged across multiple days. Resorts with 36+ holes and on-site spas are natural fits.

What This Means for Planning

The era of the one-size-fits-all golf trip is fading. The best trips are increasingly designed around the specific group — its composition, its priorities, its pace — rather than around a standardized template. The practical implication: before booking anything, define who's actually going, what each person wants from the trip, and how many hours per day should be spent on golf versus everything else. The courses will follow naturally from those answers.
