The Traveling Golfer's Tech Stack — Tools That Improve Any Trip

← All Articles

Golf travel has a technology problem — not a lack of it, but a surplus. Between GPS apps, booking platforms, swing analyzers, and tracking devices, the options are vast and overlapping. Here's what actually earns a place in the travel bag, based on utility rather than novelty.

On-Course Tools

GPS and Rangefinder Apps

18Birdies, Golfshot, and TheGrint are the three most widely used GPS apps among traveling golfers. All provide yardages, hole layouts, and shot tracking. TheGrint adds a handicap-tracking component that's useful for players maintaining a USGA index. Key distinction: many apps require a data connection for full functionality. Download course maps before arriving — cell service at remote courses (especially in the Canadian Rockies and desert regions) is unreliable. A standalone laser rangefinder serves as a reliable backup that doesn't depend on battery life or signal.

Weather Apps

Windy.app provides wind forecasts with enough granularity to influence club selection decisions hours in advance. The free tier is sufficient for most travelers. For mountain and desert golf, where conditions can shift rapidly, a quick check at the turn can meaningfully affect back-nine strategy.

Booking and Planning Tools

Tee Time Aggregators

GolfNow, TeeOff, and Chronogolf aggregate tee times across thousands of North American courses. They're useful for price comparison and last-minute bookings, but they don't always show full availability — courses often hold back premium times for direct bookings or resort guests. For bucket-list courses, always check the course's own website first; aggregators are best used for filling out the less critical rounds on a trip itinerary.

Itinerary Management

A shared Google Doc or a dedicated trip-planning app (TripIt, Wanderlog) that all travelers can access prevents the "who booked what when" confusion that plagues multi-person trips. Include confirmation numbers, tee times, addresses, and driving times between locations. Offline access matters — download everything before departing.

Gear That Travels Well

  • Bluetooth speaker: Courses that allow music are increasingly common. A compact, waterproof speaker that clips to the golf bag is practical and unobtrusive. Check the course's policy at check-in.
  • Power bank: GPS apps drain phone batteries quickly. A compact 10,000mAh power bank ensures the phone lasts through 18 holes and the post-round logistics.
  • Laser rangefinder: More reliable than GPS in areas with poor cell coverage. Models with slope adjustment provide elevation-adjusted distances that are valuable on unfamiliar terrain.
  • Tracking device: Placed in the golf bag during air travel, a Bluetooth or GPS tracker provides peace of mind and practical utility if the bag is misrouted. Apple AirTag and Tile are the most common options.

What to Skip

Swing analyzers and launch monitors that require setup time rarely justify the luggage space on a trip. The same goes for elaborate camera rigs — current-generation smartphones capture course photography that's more than sufficient for personal use. The most valuable tech on a golf trip is the stuff that solves problems, not the stuff that creates new ones.
