Sustainable Golf Travel — How to Make Better Choices Without Sacrificing Experience

← All Articles

Golf and sustainability haven't always been comfortable partners. The sport requires land, water, and maintenance inputs that can strain local resources — particularly in desert environments. But the conversation is shifting. Courses are adopting more efficient practices, travelers are asking better questions, and the industry is recognizing that the long-term viability of golf destinations depends on how they're managed today.

What Courses Are Doing

Many of the courses travelers seek out are already operating with sustainability embedded in their design and maintenance. Audubon Cooperative Sanctuary certification — a program that helps courses protect wildlife habitat, conserve water, and reduce chemical use — covers over 2,000 courses in North America, including Torrey Pines, Bandon Dunes, and dozens of municipal and resort courses. In the Coachella Valley, where water use is the defining environmental challenge, courses have transitioned to drought-tolerant turfgrasses and precision irrigation systems that reduce consumption by 20–40% compared to a decade ago. In the Canadian Rockies, courses operate within national park boundaries, subject to environmental regulations that limit expansion, chemical use, and habitat disruption.

What Travelers Can Do

  • Choose courses with environmental certifications. Audubon certification, GEO Foundation recognition, and local water stewardship programs are indicators that a course takes resource management seriously. These certifications are typically listed on course websites.
  • Play during the natural growing season. Playing desert courses in summer (when they require the most water to stay green) has a higher environmental footprint than playing them in winter, when cooler temperatures reduce irrigation demand. The financial incentives already point in this direction — the environmental alignment is a bonus.
  • Reduce vehicle emissions where practical. Walking instead of taking a cart eliminates the largest single source of a round's carbon footprint (cart batteries and electricity). Group transportation to and from courses — a single shuttle instead of four rental cars — compounds the reduction across the trip.
  • Support courses that use reclaimed water. Many desert courses in Palm Springs and Las Vegas irrigate entirely with treated wastewater rather than drawing from freshwater sources. This information is rarely advertised but is available if you ask.

The Landscape-Level Perspective

Golf courses, for all their resource demands, also preserve open space. In urbanizing regions of Southern California and the Southwest, courses function as de facto wildlife corridors and green spaces that would otherwise be developed. The net environmental impact of a golf course versus a housing development or shopping center is a more nuanced calculation than the sport's reputation sometimes suggests. This isn't an argument for golf as an environmental good — it's context for understanding the real trade-offs involved in any land-use decision.

Bottom Line

Sustainable golf travel isn't about perfection. It's about making incrementally better choices — choosing a certified course over a non-certified one, walking when possible, playing in seasons that align with natural water availability — that collectively reduce the footprint of a trip without reducing its quality. The courses doing the work deserve the business. The ones that aren't will eventually be forced to by regulation or resource reality. Travelers who choose accordingly accelerate that shift.
