Planning Your First Golf Trip to Western North America — A Decision Framework

← All Articles

The hardest part of planning a golf trip isn't finding courses. It's making the sequence of decisions that turn a vague idea into an actual itinerary. Here is a framework designed to move from general intention to specific booking, in order, without skipping the steps that cause most trips to unravel.

Step 1: Define the Group (Before Any Destination)

The single most important variable in any golf trip is the group composition. Every subsequent decision — destination, budget, courses, pacing — depends on who's going. Answer these questions first:

  • How many people? (4 or fewer = maximum flexibility. 8+ = group logistics dominate every decision.)
  • What's the skill range? (A group with handicaps ranging from 5 to 30 needs different courses than a group clustered between 8 and 15.)
  • What's the shared priority? (Maximum golf? Balanced golf + other experiences? Relaxation with some golf?)
  • Is anyone not playing golf? (This changes destination selection completely — non-golfers need a reason to be there.)

Step 2: Choose the Season (Before Any Course)

The calendar determines the destination, not the other way around. California's coast and desert have opposite peak seasons. The Canadian Rockies have a 5-month window. Forcing a destination into the wrong season guarantees a compromised experience. The question isn't "where do I want to go?" — it's "where is good when I can travel?"

Step 3: Set the Real Budget

Estimate using the all-in framework: green fees + lodging + transportation + meals + incidentals. A common mistake is budgeting for green fees only and being surprised by the rest. For a 7-day trip to California or the Southwest, a realistic per-person range (excluding transpacific flights) is $2,500–$7,000 depending on tier. The framework in Article 10 provides a detailed breakdown.

Step 4: Pick a Home Base

Moving hotels every night is the fastest way to exhaust a group. Pick one or two home bases and day-trip to courses. Los Angeles, San Diego, Palm Springs, and Las Vegas all function as multi-day bases with dozens of courses within an hour's drive. Banff/Canmore works the same way for the Canadian Rockies. Two bases in a 7-day trip is reasonable; three or more is usually excessive.

Step 5: Build the Daily Cadence

Map out each day before booking anything. A sustainable daily structure for a multi-day trip:

  • Golf-heavy day: 7:00–8:00 AM tee time, finish by 12:00 PM, afternoon free (spa, pool, sightseeing), dinner
  • Balanced day: Late morning activity, 1:00–2:00 PM tee time, casual dinner
  • Rest day: No golf. Every trip longer than 4 days needs at least one.

Step 6: Book in This Order

  1. Flights — sets the dates.
  2. Lodging — secures the home base(s).
  3. Priority tee times — the one or two bucket-list rounds that define the trip.
  4. Remaining tee times — fill the other days with complementary courses.
  5. Rental car — book early for best rates and vehicle availability.
  6. Restaurants and activities — reserve anything that requires advance booking (fine dining, spa, stadium tours).

Follow this sequence, and the trip builds itself. Skip steps, and the gaps become obvious — usually when it's too late to fix them.
