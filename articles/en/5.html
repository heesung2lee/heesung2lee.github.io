Golf Culture Shock — What International Visitors Should Know Before Playing in North America

← All Articles

Golf is played by the same rules everywhere, but how it's experienced varies dramatically by region. For international visitors — particularly from countries where golf is a high-service, caddie-driven experience — the self-service culture of North American courses can be genuinely disorienting. Understanding these differences in advance turns potential frustration into a smoother, more enjoyable trip.

1. The Self-Service Model

At roughly 95% of North American courses, the following does not exist: a caddie who carries your bag, a staff member who loads your clubs onto the cart, anyone who cleans your clubs after the round, or a designated person tracking your ball. The golfer handles all of this. The adjustment is real, but it's also liberating — you control your pace, your club selection, and your round entirely on your own terms. GPS apps and rangefinders become essential tools on unfamiliar courses.

2. Pace of Play Expectations

The standard pace on North American public courses is 4 hours to 4 hours and 30 minutes for 18 holes. Courses employ marshals who monitor pace and may ask slower groups to skip a hole or speed up. This is not a negotiation — it's a standard practice. The cultural expectation is that every group contributes to maintaining flow. Practical adjustments: limit practice swings, play ready golf (hit when ready, not strictly by honor), and keep pre-putt routines efficient.

3. Green Etiquette and Course Care

The unwritten rule on North American greens: you repair not only your own ball marks but any you notice. Divots in the fairway should be replaced or filled with seed mix (provided on carts). Bunkers are raked by the player who enters them. These are collective responsibilities — the course is maintained as much by the golfers playing it as by the grounds crew.

4. Tipping Culture

Even without caddies, North American golf includes several tipping touchpoints. Bag drop attendant: $3–5. Cart service/beverage cart: 10–15% of purchase. Post-round club cleaning: $5–10. Restaurant/bar: 18–20%. Carrying small bills is practical. Tipping is not mandatory everywhere but is widely expected at premium and resort courses.

5. Alcohol and Golf Cart Operation

Alcohol sales on the course are common — most courses have beverage carts and some have on-course bars. However, operating a golf cart under the influence is treated seriously. In many jurisdictions, it constitutes a DUI offense with legal consequences. The practical guideline: if you're drinking, let someone else drive the cart. This is not a gray area.

6. The Post-Round Experience

In many countries, the post-round meal or drinking session is an integral part of the golf day. In North America, the 19th hole is typically more casual — a drink and a snack, rarely a full sit-down meal. Most golfers finish their round, maybe grab a beer, and head out. Planning a large group dinner at the clubhouse requires advance coordination; most courses don't operate full-service restaurants in the evening.
